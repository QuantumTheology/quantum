The central thesis

Why Christianity?

The Christian case is cumulative. It does not depend on one scientific discovery or one philosophical argument.

The S-GUOT Thesis

The Seibert Grand Unified Ontological Theory examines whether a reality grounded in a personal, rational, morally perfect Creator explains the universe more coherently than worldviews that treat reality as ultimately impersonal, self-existing, or dependent on finite beings.

Central Christian claim: The universe is created by God; human beings possess real moral significance; and Jesus Christ is the incarnate Son of God whose death and resurrection are the foundation of Christian salvation.
Method: Examine each worldview by its explanation of existence, the origin and reliability of reason, consciousness, morality, evil, and the historical claims surrounding Jesus.
Worldview examination

Competing explanations of reality

Each worldview is examined by its central claims, philosophical challenges, and the Christian response.

Worldview 01

Atheism & Naturalism

MaterialismCosmologyConsciousnessMorality

Central claim: Reality is ultimately physical, and no God exists.

Evidence examined: The universe's expansion, the past-boundary implications of the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem, the existence of mathematical laws, the hard problem of consciousness, and debates about objective morality.
Christian argument: Even a complete physical description of the universe leaves philosophical questions about why physical reality exists, why it is mathematically intelligible, and how subjective consciousness and objective moral obligations fit into a fundamentally impersonal reality.
Important distinction: These are serious explanatory challenges, not demonstrations that every atheist position is logically impossible. Naturalists offer responses involving brute facts, physicalism, emergent consciousness, and moral realism. The debate must address those responses directly.
Worldview 02

Pantheism

God and universeIdentityChange

Central claim: God and the universe are identical, or all reality is divine.

Evidence examined: The universe contains change, decay, physical distinctions, and apparently contingent objects. Classical theism describes God as necessary and not dependent on the physical universe.
Christian argument: If God is identical to the changing physical universe, pantheism must explain how ultimate divinity relates to change, imperfection, and suffering. Christianity distinguishes the Creator from creation.
Important distinction: Not every form of pantheism defines God as a single unchanging object. Some versions distinguish ultimate reality from the changing world. Those versions require their own examination.
Worldview 03

Hindu Philosophical Traditions

BrahmanKarmaReincarnation

Central claim: Hindu traditions contain different accounts of ultimate reality, the self, karma, and liberation.

Evidence examined: Advaita Vedanta, Dvaita Vedanta, and other Hindu schools disagree about whether the individual self is ultimately identical with Brahman, distinct from Brahman, or related to ultimate reality in another way.
Christian argument: Christianity challenges accounts in which personal identity is ultimately dissolved or in which salvation depends on escaping the cycle of rebirth. It instead argues for a personal Creator, enduring human identity, resurrection, and a final moral judgment.
Important distinction: Hinduism is not one uniform worldview. Arguments against Advaita do not automatically refute every Hindu tradition.
Worldview 04

Buddhism

AnattaKarmaSamsara

Central claim: Buddhist traditions explain suffering through craving, impermanence, and ignorance, with liberation through awakening.

Evidence examined: The doctrine of anatta denies a permanent, independent self. Buddhist traditions explain continuity across lives through causal processes rather than an unchanging soul.
Christian argument: Christianity disagrees with the denial of a permanent self and with rebirth as the explanation of human destiny. It argues that personal identity, moral responsibility, and the hope of resurrection are grounded in a personal God.
Important distinction: Buddhism has developed multiple schools, and its account of rebirth is not simply the claim that an identical soul moves from one body to another. The Christian critique must address the actual Buddhist position.
Worldview 05

Islam

TawhidQuranJesus

Central claim: There is one God, and Muhammad is His messenger. Jesus is honored as a prophet and Messiah, but not as God.

Evidence examined: The Quran's statements about earlier revelation, its account of Jesus, the historical evidence for the crucifixion, and the development and transmission of biblical and Quranic texts.
Christian argument: Christianity argues that Jesus' identity cannot be reduced to that of a prophet. The earliest Christian sources place extraordinary claims about Jesus at the center of their message, including His crucifixion, resurrection, divine status, and role in salvation.
Important distinction: The relationship between the Quran and earlier scriptures is debated. The Islamic response includes arguments about textual corruption and interpretation. Those arguments must be examined passage by passage.
Worldview 06

Deism

First causeCreatorRevelation

Central claim: A creator exists but does not necessarily intervene in the universe or reveal Himself through specific historical events.

Evidence examined: Cosmological arguments can support a first cause or necessary foundation of reality, but additional historical and philosophical arguments are needed to establish revelation.
Christian argument: If God is personal, powerful, and morally good, divine communication is not inherently impossible. Christianity claims that God has acted in history, most decisively through Jesus.

The central Christian claim

Why Jesus is claimed to be God

The question is not merely whether God exists. It is whether Jesus is the divine Son of God.

1. Jesus' identity in the New Testament

The Gospel of John opens by describing the Word as existing in the beginning, being with God, and being God. John then identifies the Word as becoming flesh.

Primary texts: John 1:1–14; John 8:58; John 10:30; John 17:5; Colossians 2:9; Hebrews 1:1–12.
Christian interpretation: These passages are understood as presenting Jesus as more than a prophet or moral teacher: He shares divine identity while becoming genuinely human.

2. The crucifixion

Jesus' crucifixion under Pontius Pilate is one of the most widely accepted facts about His life among historians. It is reported in early Christian sources and referenced by non-Christian ancient writers.

Sources to examine: Tacitus, Annals 15.44; Josephus, Antiquities 18.3.3 (with scholarly discussion of the passage's textual history); the Pauline letters and the canonical Gospels.

3. The resurrection claim

The earliest Christians proclaimed that Jesus had been raised from the dead. The central historical questions include the disciples' experiences, the origin of resurrection belief, Paul's conversion, and James' conversion.

Primary text: 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 preserves an early tradition concerning Jesus' death, burial, resurrection, and appearances.
Christian argument: Christians argue that the resurrection best explains the origin of the early Christian movement and the disciples' conviction that Jesus was alive.
Historical distinction: Historians can investigate testimony, experiences, and the origins of belief. Whether a miracle occurred also involves philosophical assumptions about God's existence and the possibility of divine action.

4. The Christian argument for the God-man

Step one: Christianity teaches that humanity is morally accountable to a perfectly holy God.

Step two: Human beings cannot earn their way into salvation through perfect moral performance.

Step three: Christianity teaches that God acts in grace rather than abandoning justice.

Step four: Jesus is understood as fully human, representing humanity, and fully divine, revealing God and accomplishing salvation.

Primary texts: John 1:1–14; Romans 3:21–26; Hebrews 2:14–17; Philippians 2:5–11; Colossians 2:9.
Comparative theology

Christianity and Islam

A focused examination of the central disagreements between the two faiths.

1. The identity of Jesus

Christianity teaches that Jesus is the incarnate Son of God. Islam honors Jesus as Messiah and prophet but rejects His divinity.

Texts: John 1:1–14; John 20:28; Colossians 2:9; Quran 4:171; Quran 5:72–75.
Christian argument: The New Testament's earliest theological claims about Jesus create a major disagreement with the Quran's later account. The historical reliability and interpretation of these sources are central to evaluating the disagreement.

2. The crucifixion disagreement

The mainstream Islamic interpretation of Quran 4:157 denies that Jesus was crucified in the way Christians believe. Christianity places the crucifixion at the center of its historical and theological message.

Evidence to examine: Paul's letters, the four Gospels, Tacitus' Annals 15.44, and the historical scholarship on Jesus' execution.
Christian argument: If Jesus was crucified, as the strong historical consensus holds, an interpretation denying the crucifixion faces a substantial historical challenge.

3. Earlier revelation and the Bible

The Quran refers to the Torah and Gospel. Christian-Muslim debate concerns what those references mean and whether the Quran affirms the biblical texts available in its time.

Texts: Quran 3:3; 5:47; 5:68; 10:94; 18:27.
Christian argument: Christians argue that the manuscript history of the New Testament provides substantial evidence that its central claims were not simply invented centuries later.
Important distinction: Manuscript evidence demonstrates textual transmission and variation. It does not by itself prove every event described in the New Testament.

4. Salvation and assurance

Christianity teaches salvation through God's grace, received through faith in Christ. Islamic theology has different accounts of divine mercy, judgment, faith, and deeds.

Texts: Ephesians 2:8–10; Romans 3:21–26; Quran 23:102–103; Quran 39:53.
Christian argument: The Christian message emphasizes assurance grounded in Christ's completed work rather than confidence in one's own ability to accumulate sufficient merit.
